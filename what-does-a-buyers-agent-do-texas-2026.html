What does a buyer's agent actually do in Texas in 2026?

By Cristian Velez · Licensed Realtor, Corey Simpson & Associates · Principal, Elite Offer Group

If you have been shopping for a home in DFW and wondering whether you actually need a buyer's agent, or what one even does for you, this post is a direct answer to that question. The rules around buyer representation changed significantly in 2024 and 2026, and there is a lot of confusion in the market about what buyers are actually entitled to and what they are actually paying for.

Here is a clear breakdown of what a buyer's agent does, how they get paid in Texas right now, and what to look for when choosing one.

What a buyer's agent is and is not

A buyer's agent is a licensed real estate professional whose job is to represent your interests as a buyer in a transaction. That means they work for you, not for the seller, not for the listing agent, and not for the builder if you are buying new construction.

In Texas, a single agent cannot represent both the buyer and the seller in the same transaction, which is now codified in state law. If an agent ever suggests they can represent both sides, that is a problem.

A buyer's agent is not a tour guide. Their value is not in unlocking doors. Their value is in what happens before you make an offer, during negotiation, and between contract and close.

What changed in 2024 and 2026

Two things changed the buyer's agent landscape significantly.

First, the NAR settlement took effect in August 2024. The system that existed for decades, where the seller's listing agreement automatically funded the buyer's agent, is gone. Buyer agent compensation is now negotiated separately. Sellers can still choose to offer a contribution toward buyer agent fees, and most do, but it is no longer automatic or listed in the MLS.

Second, Texas went further with its own statutory mandate effective January 1, 2026, under Senate Bill 1968, which amended the Texas Real Estate License Act to require written buyer representation agreements statewide. This means every licensed Texas agent must present you with a written buyer representation agreement before showing you a single property.

That agreement defines what your agent does, what they charge, and who pays. Read it before you sign it.

What a buyer's agent actually does

Here is what you are actually getting when you work with a competent buyer's agent in DFW:

Property evaluation. A good agent helps you evaluate a property before you make an offer, not just whether you like the finishes, but what the comparable sales support in terms of pricing, what deferred maintenance is visible, and what the property's condition could mean for your offer strategy. In a market where a home can look good and still be overpriced by thirty thousand dollars, that evaluation matters.

Offer strategy. Writing a competitive offer in DFW requires more than filling out a contract. It requires knowing what the seller's situation is, how long the property has been sitting, what other offers look like if there are any, and how to structure your terms to get accepted without overpaying. That is strategy, not paperwork.

Negotiation. The offer gets accepted and then the inspection happens during the option period. What comes back on an inspection report and how you handle it is where money is either left on the table or recovered. A buyer's agent who understands repair costs, knows how to read a report, and negotiates concessions rather than just asking to walk away is doing something most buyers cannot do alone.

Contract to close management. From executed contract to closing day, there are deadlines, lender requirements, title issues, and coordination with multiple parties. A buyer's agent manages that timeline so that the transaction does not fall apart due to a missed deadline or a miscommunication between parties.

Market knowledge. In a metro as large and varied as DFW, knowing that Garland and Frisco are completely different markets at completely different price points, with completely different buyer pools, is not something a first-time buyer should have to figure out alone. A buyer's agent with genuine local knowledge can save you from buying in the wrong submarket for your goals.

How buyer's agents get paid in Texas in 2026

Most Texas closings in 2025 and 2026 still include a seller contribution toward the buyer's agent fee. The seller offering to cover the buyer's agent compensation is still the most common structure in DFW, even after the NAR settlement changed how that offer is communicated. It just happens through a different mechanism now, typically as a seller concession negotiated at the time of offer rather than a pre-listed MLS field.

In practice, most buyers in DFW are not writing a check to their agent out of pocket at closing. But you need to understand your buyer representation agreement before you start touring homes, so that the compensation structure is clear before you fall in love with a property.

What to look for in a buyer's agent

Transaction experience matters more than years licensed. An agent who has been licensed for ten years but closes five deals a year is less useful to you than an agent who has been licensed for two years and has been involved in two hundred transactions across retail and investment deals. Ask specifically about closed transactions, not tenure.

Investment literacy is underrated for any buyer. Even if you are buying a primary residence, working with an agent who understands value, repair costs, and market positioning means you are less likely to overpay and more likely to negotiate effectively. An agent who only thinks about finding you a home you love is only doing half the job.

Local submarket knowledge is specific, not general. DFW is five million people across dozens of distinct submarkets. An agent who knows Plano and McKinney well is not automatically qualified to advise you on Grand Prairie or Garland. Ask where they actually work and where their closed transactions are.

A recent example from DFW

Earlier this year I represented a VA buyer on the purchase of 517 Hanover Drive in Allen, Texas. The property was listed at $330,000. The seller dropped to $310,000 the same day we got under contract, and we negotiated a final purchase price of $305,000, plus $6,500 in seller concessions, with the washer, dryer, and refrigerator included. The buyer paid less than list price on a property that could have easily sold at full price with less experienced representation.

That outcome came from knowing when to push, what to ask for, and how to structure the request so the seller said yes rather than walking away from the negotiation entirely.

The bottom line

A buyer's agent in Texas in 2026 is not a free service the way it was sold for decades. It is a negotiated representation with a written agreement that defines the terms. What you are actually paying for, directly or through seller concessions, is access to someone who can evaluate property, negotiate on your behalf, manage a complex transaction, and bring genuine market knowledge to every step of the process.
