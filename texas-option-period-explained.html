The Texas option period explained: what it protects you from and how to use it

By Cristian Velez · Licensed Realtor, Corey Simpson & Associates · Principal, Elite Offer Group

If you are buying a home in Texas for the first time, the option period is one of the most important features of the purchase contract to understand. Buyers relocating from other states often have no equivalent in their prior experience, which leads to either undervaluing it or not using it correctly. Done right, it is one of the strongest consumer protections in any residential real estate contract in the country. Here is exactly how it works.

What the option period is

The option period is a negotiated window of time after your offer is accepted during which you have an unrestricted right to terminate the contract for any reason and receive your earnest money back. It is purchased for a fee, typically between $100 and $500 depending on the property and market conditions, paid directly to the seller at the time the contract is executed.

The word unrestricted is what makes it powerful. You do not need a reason to walk away during the option period. You do not need the inspection to reveal a specific defect. You do not need to cite a contractual contingency. If you change your mind for any reason during the option window, you notify the seller in writing before the deadline and your earnest money is returned to you. The only money you forfeit is the option fee itself.

The option period is a feature specific to Texas residential real estate. Buyers coming from California, New York, Illinois, or most other states will not have encountered anything that functions quite the same way. In those states, backing out of a contract typically requires invoking a specific contingency, which means you need a defined reason. Texas gives you a clean exit window with no justification required.

How long the option period lasts and what it costs

The length of the option period and the fee are both negotiated as part of the purchase contract. There is no fixed standard, though typical option periods in DFW run five to ten days. In a competitive multiple-offer situation, buyers sometimes offer a shorter option period to make their offer more attractive to sellers. In a buyer-favorable market like the current DFW environment, buyers have more room to negotiate a longer window.

The option fee is paid directly to the seller and is non-refundable regardless of what happens. If you terminate during the option period, you get your earnest money back but the seller keeps the option fee. If you close on the property, the option fee is typically credited toward your closing costs or purchase price depending on how the contract is written.

Option fees in DFW currently run between $100 and $500 for most residential transactions in the $300,000 to $700,000 range. On higher-priced properties or in competitive situations, sellers sometimes push for higher option fees as a signal of buyer commitment. Your agent should advise you on what is appropriate for the specific property and market conditions.

What you should do during the option period

The option period is your due diligence window. The single most important thing to do is schedule and complete a professional home inspection as early as possible after the contract is executed. Do not wait until day four or five of a seven-day option period. Schedule the inspection for day one or two so you have maximum time to review the findings, request additional inspections if needed, and negotiate any repairs or credits before the window closes.

A standard home inspection in Texas covers the foundation, roof, HVAC systems, plumbing, electrical, and all major systems and appliances. In DFW specifically, foundation inspection deserves particular attention given the expansive clay soils common throughout North Texas. If your general inspector notes anything concerning about the foundation, order a separate structural engineer evaluation before your option period expires. Foundation repairs in this market range from minor to extremely costly, and knowing what you are dealing with before you commit is essential.

If the inspection reveals significant issues, you have three options: negotiate with the seller for repairs or a credit, accept the property as-is, or terminate the contract and recover your earnest money. Most transactions involve some negotiation after the inspection. Your agent's job during this stage is to help you assess which findings are worth negotiating and how to structure the request in a way that keeps the deal together while protecting your interests.

Beyond the general inspection, use the option period to verify anything else that matters to your decision. Drive the neighborhood at different times of day. Confirm school district boundaries directly with the district if that is a factor. Review HOA documents if the property is in a managed community. The option period is your last no-cost exit, and the time is better spent doing thorough due diligence than assuming everything will be fine.

Common mistakes buyers make with the option period

Waiting too long to schedule the inspection is the most common mistake. Inspectors in DFW book out quickly, particularly during active buying seasons. If you execute a contract on Monday and start calling inspectors Tuesday afternoon, you may not get an appointment until day four or five of a seven-day option period. That compresses your time to review, negotiate, and make a decision uncomfortably.

Terminating over minor inspection findings is a mistake on the other end of the spectrum. Every home, regardless of age or condition, will produce an inspection report with a list of items. The question is not whether issues exist but whether they are material enough to affect your decision or warrant negotiation. An agent who has reviewed many inspection reports can help you distinguish between findings that matter and those that are standard maintenance items not worth blowing up a transaction over.

Missing the option period deadline is the most serious mistake and one with real financial consequences. If you fail to terminate in writing before the option period expires and later decide you do not want to proceed, you are no longer protected by the option. Walking away after the option period without invoking another contractual contingency puts your earnest money at risk. The deadline is not a suggestion. Track it carefully.

How the option period fits into the broader Texas purchase process

The option period sits between contract execution and the formal closing process. Once the option period expires and you have decided to proceed, the transaction moves into the loan processing and appraisal phase. Your lender orders the appraisal, processes your documentation, and works toward a clear-to-close. The title company coordinates the closing details. You are still protected by your financing contingency if you included one, but the unrestricted exit window is closed.

Understanding where the option period fits in the overall timeline helps you plan your due diligence activities correctly. Everything that requires a no-questions-asked exit should happen during the option period. Decisions made after it closes carry more financial risk if the transaction falls apart.
