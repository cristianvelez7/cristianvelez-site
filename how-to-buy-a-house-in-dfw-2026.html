How to buy a house in DFW in 2026: a straightforward guide

By Cristian Velez · Licensed Realtor, Corey Simpson & Associates · Principal, Elite Offer Group

Buying a house in DFW in 2026 is a different process than it was in 2021 or even 2023. The market has normalized, rates are higher than the post-pandemic lows, and the rules around buyer representation changed in 2025. If you are starting from scratch or coming back to the market after time away, here is a clear walkthrough of how the process actually works today.

Step 1: Get your financing in order before you look at houses

This step comes first for a reason. In the DFW market, sellers take offers more seriously when you have a pre-approval letter from a lender, not just a pre-qualification. The difference matters: a pre-qualification is a quick estimate based on what you told a lender, while a pre-approval means a lender has reviewed your income, assets, and credit and issued a conditional commitment to lend.

Get pre-approved before you start touring homes. It tells you what you can actually afford, which saves time and prevents the frustration of falling for a house outside your range. It also puts you in a position to move quickly when you find the right property, which still matters even in a more balanced market.

If you are considering a rate buydown, an FHA loan, a VA loan, or a USDA loan, make sure your lender has real experience with that product. The timelines and requirements vary, and a lender who routinely handles your loan type will close faster with fewer surprises.

Step 2: Understand the buyer representation rules that changed in 2025

Texas Senate Bill 1968, which took effect in 2025 alongside the NAR settlement, changed how buyer agent compensation works. The short version: you are now required to sign a written buyer representation agreement with a licensed agent before they can show you property. That agreement spells out what the agent will be paid and by whom.

This is not as complicated as it sounds. In most DFW transactions, sellers are still offering to cover buyer agent compensation as part of the deal, but it is now negotiated and disclosed explicitly rather than buried in MLS commission fields. You should read the agreement before you sign it and understand what you are committing to.

The practical implication is that you should choose your agent before you start touring homes, not after you find a house you want to write an offer on. Signing a representation agreement when you are already emotionally attached to a property is not the right time to be evaluating who is in your corner.

Step 3: Define what you are looking for with precision

The DFW metro covers a large geographic area with significant price variation between submarkets. Frisco and McKinney are different markets from Garland and Mesquite, and Grand Prairie is different from Southlake. Before you start seriously searching, get clear on the variables that actually matter for your situation: commute tolerance, school district requirements, price ceiling, minimum square footage, and whether new construction or resale is on the table.

The more precise your criteria, the faster the search moves. An agent who knows DFW well can tell you quickly which submarkets fit your criteria and which ones to deprioritize. That narrows the search from hundreds of active listings to a manageable set worth your time.

Step 4: Evaluate properties with the right framework

When you tour a property, you are evaluating it on two levels simultaneously. The first is whether it fits your needs. The second is whether the price reflects the market. These are separate questions and both matter.

On the pricing side, your agent should pull recent comparable sales before you make any offer. Comparable sales are closed transactions of similar properties in the same area, usually within the past three to six months. That data tells you what the market has actually been willing to pay, which is different from what a seller is asking.

In the current DFW market, homes that are priced accurately are selling at or close to list price. Homes that are overpriced are sitting, accumulating days on market, and eventually being reduced. Days on market is a useful signal. A property that has been listed for 60 or 90 days without going under contract is telling you something about price or condition.

Step 5: Structure the offer strategically

Writing an offer is not just about the price. The terms of your offer, including the option period, earnest money amount, closing timeline, financing contingency, and any seller concessions you request, all affect how a seller evaluates your offer relative to others.

In a situation where you are competing against other buyers, those terms matter. A shorter option period, a larger earnest money deposit, and a flexible closing date can make your offer more competitive without changing the price. Conversely, in a situation where a property has been sitting and the seller is motivated, you have room to negotiate price reductions, concessions toward closing costs or a rate buydown, and repairs identified during inspection.

The option period in Texas is negotiated, typically three to seven days for resale properties. During that window, you can terminate the contract for any reason and get your earnest money back. Use that time to complete your inspection and make decisions based on what it finds, not on assumptions.

Step 6: Navigate the contract period

Once you are under contract, the process moves through a defined sequence: option period, inspection, appraisal if you are financing, title work, final walkthrough, and closing. Each step has deadlines and requirements. Missing a deadline can cost you your earnest money or the deal itself.

The inspection is the most important step in the option period. Hire a licensed inspector, read the report, and focus on the items that affect safety, structure, and systems rather than cosmetic issues. Use the findings to renegotiate if the property has material defects the seller did not disclose, or to exit the contract if the issues are severe enough that the deal no longer makes sense.

If your offer included a financing contingency, your lender will order an appraisal during this period. If the property appraises below the purchase price, you have options: renegotiate the price, make up the difference in cash, or exit the contract if your agreement includes an appraisal contingency. Work this out with your agent before it becomes a problem.

What the DFW market looks like for buyers in late 2026

The DFW market in late 2026 is more buyer-friendly than it was at the peak in 2021 and 2022, but it is not a buyer's market in most submarkets. Active inventory has increased from the historic lows of that period, giving buyers more options and more time to evaluate. Days on market has normalized to the 25-45 day range for most price points. Seller concessions are available and being negotiated, particularly rate buydowns in the new construction market.

That said, well-priced properties in desirable areas still move. The window to tour, evaluate, and make a decision is longer than it was, but it is not unlimited. The buyers who are doing well right now are the ones who did their preparation early, know their criteria clearly, and are ready to act when the right property shows up.
