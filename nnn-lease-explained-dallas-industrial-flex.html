NNN leases explained: what a $15 per square foot rate really costs a Dallas flex tenant

By Cristian Velez · Licensed Realtor, Corey Simpson & Associates · Principal, Elite Offer Group

If you are shopping for warehouse or flex space, the number on the flyer is not the number you will write a check for each month. Industrial space is usually quoted on a triple net basis, and the gap between the quoted rate and your true occupancy cost catches first-time tenants off guard. Here is how it works, using a real listing I am currently marketing: a 7,200 SF climate-controlled industrial/flex building at 2647 Mañana Dr in Northwest Dallas.

What NNN actually means

On a triple net lease, you pay base rent plus your share of the property's operating costs. Those costs are typically property taxes, building insurance, and common area maintenance. The landlord quotes them as a per-square-foot estimate, and you pay that estimate monthly alongside rent. After the year closes, the landlord reconciles the estimate against actual costs, and you either owe the difference or receive a credit.

That is different from a gross lease, where one number covers everything, and from a modified gross lease, where the landlord and tenant split some costs. Neither structure is better in the abstract. NNN gives the landlord predictability and gives you transparency into what the building really costs to run, but it means your monthly total will be higher than the base rate suggests.

The math on a real listing

The Mañana Dr building is 7,200 SF at $15.00 per square foot per year NNN, with estimated NNN charges of $3.85 per square foot and 5 percent annual escalations. Here is what that looks like in dollars:

ItemPer yearPer month
Base rent ($15.00/SF)$108,000$9,000
Estimated NNN ($3.85/SF)$27,720$2,310
Total, year one$135,720$11,310

Put differently, the "$15 building" costs about $18.85 per square foot once the estimated NNN charges are included. That is the number to use when comparing it against another space, including one quoted on a gross basis.

What the escalations do over time

A 5 percent annual escalation means base rent steps up every year of the lease. Assuming the increase applies to base rent and compounds, the first three years look like this, holding the NNN estimate flat for simplicity (in reality it will move with taxes, insurance, and maintenance costs):

Lease yearBase rent per SFMonthly base rentMonthly with NNN
Year 1$15.00$9,000$11,310
Year 2$15.75$9,450$11,760
Year 3$16.54$9,923$12,233

Over a three-year term that is roughly $340,000 in base rent and another $83,000 in estimated NNN charges, or about $424,000 in total. Knowing that figure up front is what lets you decide whether the space fits your revenue, not just whether the starting rate looks attractive.

Three years or five

This listing carries a three-year minimum term, with incentives available if the tenant commits to five. Landlords value longer terms because they reduce turnover and vacancy risk, so they will often trade something for the extra commitment. What that something is, whether it is free rent, an improvement allowance, or a better rate, is negotiable and varies by building and landlord.

For you, the question is how confident you are in the space. A growing business may want the flexibility of three years. A business with a stable operation and a significant build-out or move cost may be better served by five, especially if the incentive is meaningful. If you are leaning toward the longer term, ask about a renewal option as well, so you are not forced to relocate at the end.

What to ask before you sign

  • Where the NNN estimate comes from. Ask for the prior year's actual taxes, insurance, and maintenance costs, and whether the estimate reflects a recent property tax reassessment.
  • What is excluded from pass-throughs. Capital repairs, roof replacement, and structural items should be addressed clearly in the lease.
  • Who maintains the building systems. HVAC, plumbing, the loading dock, and overhead doors can be a landlord or tenant responsibility, and it changes your real costs.
  • Permitted use and zoning. Confirm your operation is allowed under the lease and under the property's zoning, and read any use restrictions closely.
  • The building specs your operation needs. Clear height, dock access, power, and outdoor space matter more to a warehouse tenant than the finishes.
  • Delivery date and condition. If the space is subject to an existing tenant vacating, understand what happens to your start date if that slips.
  • Deposits, guaranties, and assignment rights. These shape your risk far more than the rate does.

The bottom line

Compare space on total occupancy cost, not the quoted rate, and make sure you know how rent and charges will change across the full term. Commercial leases in Texas are largely a matter of negotiation, so the lease document itself is what protects you. Have an attorney review it before you sign.
